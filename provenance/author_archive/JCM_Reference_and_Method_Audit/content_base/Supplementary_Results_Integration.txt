Liver-cell localization and numerical calibration

The liver atlas located these quantities within a multicellular tissue. Descriptive embeddings documented hepatocyte, endothelial, and macrophage structure (Supplementary Fig. S12B–D). Equal-donor estimates showed prominent endothelial detection of KDR (31.9%), FLT1 (52.2%), FLT4 (16.2%), and FGFR1 (22.2%). FGFR2 and FGFR3 were most detectable in cholangiocytes; PDGFRA and PDGFRB were most evident in stellate cells, although that compartment comprised only 37 cells from four donors (Supplementary Fig. S12E,G). The injury genes were predominantly hepatocellular: detection reached 94.5% for ALDOB, 84.8% for CES1, 74.2% for CPS1, 58.6% for GSTA1, 54.7% for FBP1, and 29.9% for LECT2 (Supplementary Fig. S12F). This distribution directs the mechanistic question toward non-parenchymal receptor signaling and its effects on hepatocellular injury.

Before evaluating that connection, we audited 95731 gene-level records from 210 virtual-knockout runs (Supplementary Fig. S12H–K). In 132 runs, maximum displacement was at floating-point scale, no greater than 2.85×10−15. The smallest maximum among nonzero runs was 4.73×10−7; classifications were identical using cutoffs of 10−14, 10−12, or 10−10. Of 102 drug-target runs, 73 were numerically zero and 29 were quantifiable. Percentile normalization had assigned ordered scores even to noise-scale distances, so those runs could not support a biological perturbation claim.

FGFR1 illustrated the consequence. Only one of three endothelial runs was numerically nonzero (maximum 6.02×10−6); the other two maxima were approximately 10−15, as was its available macrophage result (Supplementary Fig. S12J). The earlier favorable percentiles therefore did not establish reproducible FGFR1 perturbation. Across all 29 quantifiable drug-target runs, projection of the continuous human DILI-versus-non-DILI protein profile did not exceed expression-matched backgrounds (minimum upper-tail P=0.352). The valid FGFR1 endothelial run had standardized difference −2.20 and P=1.000 (Supplementary Fig. S12K). Numerical calibration thus changed the target interpretation and established the need for an independently calibrated way to connect drug pharmacology to the clinical phenotype.

Network decomposition and sensitivity analyses

We propagated the continuous protein phenotype through donor-balanced shrinkage networks and evaluated target proximity against matched seed sets (Fig. 5B). The fixed 17-target universe produced 50 evaluable target–compartment combinations; FGFR2 lacked an endothelial value. All primary computations passed numerical checks, with maximum linear-system residual 8.88×10−16 across the 18 graph specifications. Four clinical-weight profiles generated 3600 candidate estimates. Five actual donor omissions generated 250 estimates, including one undefined null-adjusted value retained as unavailable.

Three primary associations reached joint-family q=0.00833: endothelial LRRK2 (Z=27.424), endothelial TYK2 (Z=5.670), and macrophage PDGFRA (Z=7.299). LRRK2 and PDGFRA also met the specified graph- and donor-rank stability requirements. Their same-compartment detection fractions were nevertheless only 4.184% and 0.139%, respectively; TYK2 detection was 5.324%. No candidate satisfied significance, stability, and the inherited 10% expression threshold together (Fig. 5C,M). The threshold was retained rather than relaxed to recover a preferred molecule.

Seed decomposition explained why apparently large network effects required this qualification. OTC and ALDOB contributed 47.43% and 45.74% of the endothelial LRRK2 score; PCK2 contributed 92.68% for endothelial TYK2, and HPD contributed 91.41% for macrophage PDGFRA (Fig. 5D). The latter receptor was detected in just two cells across two donors. Degree-matched calibration retained the endothelial associations across matching-pool sizes, but macrophage PDGFRA lost FDR significance with the 100-gene pool. Donor omissions also produced negative as well as positive Z values for the endothelial candidates (Fig. 5E). FGFR1 had negative primary null-adjusted scores in all three modeled compartments. These findings identify sparse or concentrated connections, rather than broad, expression-supported disruption of the clinical program.

Changing the clinical contrast or source cohort while retaining the graph gave candidate-rank correlations of 0.838–0.982 with the primary weighting. Unshrunk and shrinkage-conditioned ranks correlated at 0.517 in hepatocytes, 0.821 in endothelial cells, and 0.924 in macrophages, with maximum changes of 13, seven, and five positions. Thus, the human phenotype could be transported across several weighting definitions, but covariance estimation remained consequential, especially in hepatocytes. The comparison identifies covariance estimation as an influential design choice to include in subsequent experimental benchmarking.

The algorithm comparison separated rank stability from predictive evidence. Geometric fusion retained full-data ranks after component omission with mean Spearman correlation 0.833 versus 0.793 for the exact historical consensus, and mean top-five Jaccard overlap 0.607 versus 0.506 (Fig. 5H). Agreement with the omitted feature was weak, however: mean correlation 0.045 for geometric fusion, with maximin highest at 0.115 (Fig. 5I). None of the five revised methods produced a competitive q value below 0.05; minima were 0.139 for robust rank aggregation and 0.178 for geometric fusion. Numerically valid legacy virtual-knockout comparisons were also limited: requiring three evaluable seeds left only two endothelial and two macrophage candidates, insufficient for an informative broad algorithm comparison.

Single and joint PDGFRA and FLT4 network interventions

Single and joint PDGFRA/FLT4 edge deletions generated numerically consistent outputs across donor omissions. The joint deletion changed the clinical-weighted propagation score by 0.000336%, 0.02154% and 0.03848% in hepatocyte, endothelial and macrophage networks, respectively; none of the nine primary effects exceeded matched-control backgrounds after correction (all q≥0.529; Supplementary Fig. S11A–F). Joint residuals also remained nonsignificant after three-test correction (q=0.991, 0.250 and 0.101; Supplementary Fig. S11G,H). Reciprocal receptor-score redistribution was descriptive (Supplementary Fig. S11I). PDGFRA was detected in 8, 33 and 2 cells in the respective compartments, compared with 4, 150 and 13 for FLT4. The extension therefore defines model-level sensitivity and cellular-coverage requirements while retaining the original interpretation of PDGFRA and FLT4 as priorities for receptor-specific testing.

Pulmonary cellular and protein context

We retained similarly informative negative findings from more detailed pulmonary measurements. In GSE151374, 70696 cells came from 18 pooled libraries; three pools per condition and time supplied the statistical units. Neither macrophage genes, composition, nor prespecified programs showed FDR-controlled treatment effects. The day-14 receptor-tyrosine-kinase program and FGFR1 transcript changes were nominally positive, but each had exact three-versus-three P=0.10 and failed correction (Supplementary Fig. S6). In PXD052594, the unconditioned deposited-animal comparison included 10 nintedanib and 13 vehicle mice after source postrandomization losses. None of 7006 proteins passed q<0.10 (minimum q=0.608), and the prespecified programs were also null (Supplementary Fig. S7). In the separate five-versus-five phosphoproteome subset, an unmoderated parametric screen identified 994 of 20043 sites at q<0.05, with 495 decreases and 499 increases. However, no quantified site in the examined FGFR3, PDGFRA, FLT4, MAPK1, MAPK3, AKT1, or STAT3 tracks passed that threshold, and an FGFR1 site was unavailable. Small-group variance estimation, preprocessing, and unmeasured batch structure limit this table-level sensitivity; absent matched total-protein adjustment also prevents distinguishing phosphorylation changes from protein-abundance shifts. These findings did not provide target-specific functional confirmation.
